# Diction, reference, and narrative movement

## The subject gives the reader somewhere to stand

A technical noun is useful after its object is clear. “Head-cohort difference” compresses a household head, a birth year, a school-entry rule, two groups, and an outcome into three words. Start with the heads who reached school age at different times. After explaining the comparison, use a short stable name for it.

Natural prose is not simply active voice. The relevant subject can be a person, a record, an estimate, an institution, or a restriction. Choose the subject that connects what the reader already knows to the next fact. A sentence about records should usually be followed by what those records contain or permit, before moving to what residents believed.

Useful distinctions:

- A registry **records**, **lists**, or **recognizes** deaths. It does not directly **measure memory**.
- A school **bears** a name; a ledger **dates** its adoption; a facility **exists** by a child's sixth birthday. These are different facts.
- Respondents **report** attitudes. A cross-section **relates** attitudes to exposure; it does not observe a change in beliefs decades earlier.
- An estimate **falls**, **rises**, or **changes little** after a control is added. “Becomes more robust” does not say what changed.
- A coefficient **is imprecisely estimated** when its interval is wide. “Has no effect” requires a much stronger argument.

These are choices tied to the evidence, not a mandatory verb list.

## Given information comes before the new step

A reader should not have to reconstruct the previous sentence to interpret “this,” “these,” “it,” or “the pattern.” After several datasets or coefficients, repeat the particular noun. After one clearly stated comparison, “this difference” can be economical.

A sequence can move from a known fact to its implication: a law changed access; access changed the choices available; the empirical comparison separates people facing different access. Technical detail then explains how the comparison is estimated. Starting with the estimator and ending with the people reverses the order in which most readers learn the argument.

Use the end of one paragraph to create the question answered by the next. If adding a control changes the sample, the next question is whether the estimate changed because of coverage or adjustment. If basic schooling rises while later attainment does not, the next question concerns entry and progression. “Moreover” or “Turning to mechanisms” cannot supply that motivation on its own.

## Fluent rhythm is purposeful variation

Do not impose an arbitrary maximum sentence length. A sentence with two linked clauses may explain a conditional relationship better than three disconnected fragments. Follow a dense explanation with a short sentence that gives its implication. Keep one clear main action; avoid nested appositives and long strings of comma clauses.

Do not impose “We” on every sentence. “We compare” is useful when introducing a research choice. “Table 2 shows” is useful when a particular result advances the argument. “The estimate falls from ... to ...” is useful when the reader needs to assess sensitivity. “Families ...” is useful when explaining behavior. Variety comes from the argument, not from swapping synonyms.

Follow the author's punctuation and vocabulary preferences. Avoid inflated wording and repeatedly interrupted sentences. Preserve technical terms that add precision, but explain them in place. Do not replace an established term with an unfamiliar synonym for elegance.

## A narrative is a chain of reasons

A book-like explanation need not be ornate. It allows a fact to become a question, introduces an institution when it answers that question, and gives an example only when it explains an economic action. Historical scenes and household choices must come from evidence. Never invent a typical family, motive, conversation, or archival detail.

Lin's 1992 reform analysis motivates separating components by explaining why rival accounts imply different policy choices. Goldin's historical explanations often show how a household or worker's choices change before expressing the incentive in abstract terms. These are useful habits to adapt, not sentences or metaphors to copy. A lecture or book can tolerate rhetorical devices that an empirical abstract may not need.
