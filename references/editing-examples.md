# Illustrative editing examples

All settings, values and passages below are synthetic. They illustrate editing decisions, not findings from an unpublished paper or mandatory sentence templates.

## Explain the people and comparison before the estimator

Before: “We estimate the cohort gradient using an interacted treatment specification.”

After: “Children who reached school age after the program began faced different opportunities from earlier cohorts. We compare this change across districts with different levels of program exposure.”

This wording is appropriate only when those are the actual groups, timing and source of variation. The estimator can follow once the comparison is clear.

## Give the next sentence a reason to exist

Before: “We find a positive estimate. We next examine heterogeneity. We use initial access as a split.”

After: “The expansion was concentrated in basic schooling. Districts with low initial enrollment had more room to expand, so we next compare them with districts where access was already widespread.”

The transition depends on evidence for the first statement and a meaningful measure of initial access. Do not invent the motivation after seeing significant subgroups.

## Separate a change in coverage from a control adjustment

Before: “The estimate turns negative after adding the historical control.”

After, if the estimates support it: “The coefficient is already negative in districts covered by the historical source. Adding the control changes it little, suggesting that the difference from the national estimate mainly reflects sample coverage.”

Compare baseline and controlled specifications on the same observations before making this claim.

## Explain a large IV estimate constructively

Before: “IV is much larger than OLS, which may indicate a problem.”

After: “IV and OLS need not estimate the same weighted response. The instrument isolates the variation associated with the historical supply change, which may receive different weights from other sources of exposure. Measurement error could also attenuate OLS under the relevant assumptions.”

These are possible explanations, not evidence that a particular one is true. Establish common samples and units, check what variation the instrument changes, and state the specific exclusion condition where the estimate is interpreted.

## Retain supported significance and the economic comparison

Before: “The program significantly improves outcomes.”

After, for a result whose inference supports it: “Enrollment rises by an additional 0.9 percentage points in higher-exposure districts, a statistically significant difference.”

Name the exposure contrast and relevant unit in the actual paper. The lesson is to make the comparison clear, not to prohibit “significantly.” An abstract may summarize the finding without giving the transformation formula.

## Keep the concept separate from its proxy

Before: “The archive measures trust.”

After: “The archive records participation in local associations. We use it to study one setting in which residents could cooperate.”

Administrative participation, attitudes, and underlying preferences are different objects. State the relevant distinction where it matters; do not add the same disclaimer to every paragraph.

## Explain what a survey question asks

Before: “Respondents are more willing to invest in their children.”

After, for a question about determinants of success: “Respondents place more importance on education for getting ahead. The question concerns beliefs about success rather than intended spending on a child.”

The wording of the item determines the interpretation, not its short variable label.

## Anchor panels and omit filing instructions

Before: “Panel B is positive. Appendix D contains the sample definitions and further estimates.”

After: “Panel B of Table 5 shows higher enrollment in the more exposed districts.”

In the introduction, retain the substantive finding if it contributes to the argument. Put appendix references where the reader needs supporting evidence, rather than using them to announce file locations.

## Use informative uncertainty

Before: “The estimate is not significant, so there is no effect.”

After: “The confidence interval includes both a small decline and a meaningful increase. The estimate is too imprecise to distinguish between them.”

A wide interval is different from a precise estimate near zero. Neither should be concealed or converted into a more confident claim through prose.

## Keep the research action visible

Before: “The data combine school records and household surveys. Variation in reform timing permits an analysis of enrollment.”

After: “We ask how families responded to the reform. We link school records to household surveys and compare enrollment in districts reached earlier and later.”

The revision names the authors' question, data work, and comparison. The institutional background must establish what the reform changed and why timing is informative before the paragraph makes a causal claim. Varying sentence subjects is useful only when it helps this explanation.
